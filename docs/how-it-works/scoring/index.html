Scoring

Aegis produces a composite ranking score for each candidate by combining four components: a quality prior, topical fit, recency, and the integrity gate. Each component captures a different dimension of a researcher's suitability for a given specialty query.

Quality Prior​

The quality prior measures the overall strength of a researcher's profile, independent of any specific query. It answers the question: "How strong is this researcher in general?" The prior is built from six sub-scores (seven for clinicians):

  • F1 — Citation Impact: Based on the Relative Citation Ratio from iCite, which normalizes citation counts by field and publication year. A highly cited paper in a small field is valued appropriately rather than being overshadowed by papers in high-volume fields.
  • F2 — Funding Trajectory: Tracks grant activity from NIH RePORTER and OpenAlex Grants (which covers major international funders including ERC, Wellcome Trust, DFG, and others), considering total funding, number of active grants, and the progression from early-career to established investigator awards.
  • F3 — Leadership Positions: Captures editorial board memberships, society leadership roles, and other markers of professional standing in the field.
  • F4 — Apex-List Membership: Whether the researcher appears on curated apex rosters — recognized lists of top experts in specific fields, such as FDA advisory committee members or NCCN panel members.
  • F5 — Translational Output: Measures the ability to move findings from bench to bedside, incorporating clinical trial involvement, FDA drug approvals, and for drug-discovery researchers, patent filings and chemical biology contributions via ChEMBL.
  • F6 — Academic Lineage: Captures the researcher's training pedigree through the academic family tree — who they trained with and what those mentors have accomplished.
  • F7 — Clinician Score (clinician population only): Synthesizes board certification status, active medical licenses, hospital-tier affiliation, clinical-trial PI roles, and procedure volumes into a single clinician-specific metric.

These sub-scores are combined using a weighted geometric mean. The geometric mean naturally penalizes candidates who are strong in one area but weak in others — a researcher with one exceptional metric and several poor ones scores lower than a well-rounded candidate with consistently good scores across all dimensions.

Each sub-score is percentile-calibrated within its specialty cohort, ensuring that the scale is consistent across different fields where raw values may differ dramatically.

Topical Fit​

Topical fit is query-dependent — it measures how closely a candidate's body of work aligns with the specific specialty being queried. The process works in three steps:

  1. Query expansion: The specialty query is expanded into a set of MeSH (Medical Subject Headings) descriptors that capture the relevant topic space. This expansion handles synonyms and related terms so that a query for "NSCLC immunotherapy" also picks up candidates working on closely related topics.

  2. Candidate portfolio: Each candidate's publications, patents, grants, and trials are mapped to MeSH descriptors, building a vector that represents their research footprint across the topic space.

  3. Similarity: The query vector and candidate vector are compared using cosine similarity, producing a score between zero and one. A candidate whose work is tightly focused on the queried specialty scores high; a generalist whose work touches the topic peripherally scores lower.

Recency​

Recency is also query-dependent. It measures how recently a candidate has been active in the queried specialty. The score uses an exponential decay function: recent publications, grants, and trials in the relevant specialty contribute more than older ones. A researcher who published five papers in the last two years scores higher on recency than one whose last relevant publication was a decade ago.

The decay rate is tuned so that very recent work receives strong signal without completely discounting sustained careers. A researcher with steady output over 15 years still receives meaningful recency credit, while someone who left the field 8 years ago sees their score decay substantially.

Composite Score​

The four components combine into the final ranking score:

Score = Integrity x Quality^alpha x Topical Fit^beta x Recency^gamma

The exponents — alpha, beta, and gamma — control how much weight each component receives in the final ranking. These exponents are not set by hand. They are learned from data through a Plackett-Luce statistical model that fits the exponents to match expert pairwise judgments: domain experts compare pairs of candidates and indicate which should rank higher, and the model finds the exponent values that best reproduce those preferences.

Query Type Classifier​

Before scoring, Aegis classifies each incoming query into one of four query types, each mapped to a specific weight vector:

  • basic_research (default) — fundamental science queries; uses the translational weight vector
  • drug_discovery — queries mentioning drug targets, compounds, or therapeutic development; uses the drug-discovery weight vector
  • clinical_trial_pi — queries seeking clinical investigators or trial leaders; uses a clinician-weighted vector emphasizing F7 and trial PI roles
  • policy_epi — epidemiology and health policy queries; uses a vector emphasizing publication breadth and funding

The classifier uses keyword matching with confidence levels. When confidence is low, it falls back to the default basic_research type. Weight vector files are stored at config/aegis/weights/*.yaml and can be customized per deployment.

Per-Population and Per-Query-Type Weights​

Each of the three researcher populations — Translational, Drug Discovery, and Clinician — has its own weight vector. The exponents are learned independently for each population because the relative importance of scoring components differs:

  • In the Translational population, citation impact and funding trajectory carry significant weight.
  • In the Drug Discovery population, translational output (heavily driven by patents) is the dominant signal.
  • In the Clinician population, the clinician-specific F7 score and leadership roles receive substantial weight.

Weight vectors are versioned and stored in configuration files. When the learning pipeline produces updated exponents, the new weights are deployed through a controlled process with stability checks.
